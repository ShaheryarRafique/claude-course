## Lesson 1.1 slide 2 — A search box, or an assistant?

Hook: Here is the shift. Most people use Claude like a search box. It actually works far more like an assistant who does the task with you.

Talking points:
- A search box answers one query, then stops. Claude works through a whole task with you.
- A search box starts fresh every single time. Claude remembers your context and your files.
- A search box gives generic results. Claude uses your situation and your documents.
- A search box only returns links. Claude writes, builds, analyses, and takes direction.
- With a search box, you take what you get. With Claude, you steer, you correct, you improve it.
- Read the table one row at a time, left to right, so the contrast lands.
- One line worth saying: Claude is built to be helpful, honest, and harmless, and it is steerable. It takes your direction on tone and style.

Close: Keep that word in your head, an assistant, not a search box. Now let's test it.

## Lesson 1.1 slide 3 — Search box, or assistant job?

Hook: Let's play a quick game. I will read a request, and you decide: is this a search box, or a real assistant job?

Talking points and reveal (say the request, reveal the badge, then read the why):
- First: "What is the capital of Japan?" [PAUSE for the student to guess.] Search box. It is a plain fact. Nothing of yours is in it.
- Second: "Rewrite my update so the delay reads as a decision, not an apology." [PAUSE.] Assistant. It needs your draft and your judgement.
- Third, and this is the interesting one: "Best structure for a quarterly review deck?" [PAUSE.] This one looks like a search. But the moment your own slides and your own goals go in, it becomes an assistant job. That is the teaching moment. The tool did not change. Your context did.

Close on the rule (read it word for word): A plain fact belongs in a search box. Anything with your own work in it is an assistant job.

## Lesson 1.2 slide 4 — Write a good prompt

Hook: A good prompt usually has three parts. Learn these once, and you will reuse them forever.

Talking points:
- Set the stage: who you are, your goal, the context.
- Define the task: the exact action. Write, analyse, plan, build.
- Specify the rules: length, tone, format, or an example to copy.
- Point at each part on screen as you name it.

Build the running-example prompt live and read it aloud, calling out the three parts as you go:
[PROMPT]
I have my finals in three weeks. Turn my main subjects into a seven day revision plan, one topic per day, in plain language.

- Narrate it: "I have my finals in three weeks" is the stage. "Turn my main subjects into a seven day revision plan" is the task. "One topic per day, in plain language" are the rules.

## Lesson 1.3 slide 2 — Five fixes that solve most problems

Hook: Almost every disappointing answer comes down to one of five things. Here they are, with the fix for each.

Talking points:
- Too generic? Add your audience, your role, and your constraints.
- Too long or too short? State the length, for example "under 100 words".
- Wrong format? Show an example, or describe the structure you want.
- Confident but wrong? Ask for sources, and verify anything that matters.
- Wrong tone? Name the tone, or paste a sample in the voice you want.
- This is the single most useful minute for a beginner. Say each problem, then its fix.

Close: Behind all five is one mindset shift. Let's name it.
